Most differential equations that we meet in physics have no solution in closed form, and we have to solve them numerically. The pendulum is a good place to learn how. Its equation is simple, and its motion shows two features of nonlinear dynamics, a period that depends on the amplitude and a separatrix between two kinds of motion. We also know exact results for it, such as the conserved energy and the period, so we can test a numerical solution against them. In this post, we write the pendulum as a system of first-order equations, sketch its phase portrait, and solve it with `odeint` from SciPy.

We all know the simple pendulum, a bob on a light rod of length $\ell$. It swings according to

$$
\begin{align}
\dfrac{d^2 x}{d t^2} + \dfrac{g}{\ell} x = 0 ,
\end{align}
$$

where $x$ is the angle that the rod makes with the vertical. Its solutions oscillate with the period $2\pi \sqrt{\ell / g}$, whatever their amplitude. This equation is an approximation. The component of gravity along the path of the bob is $-mg \sin x$, so Newton's second law gives

$$
\begin{align}
m \ell \dfrac{d^2 x}{d t^2} = -m g \sin x ,
\end{align}
$$

and the simple pendulum follows only for small angles, where $\sin x \approx x$. If we keep the $\sin x$ and choose units of time in which $g / \ell = 1$, the motion of a nonlinear pendulum is governed by the well-known equation [\[1\]](#ref-1),

$$
\begin{align}
\dfrac{d^2 x}{d t^2} + \sin x = 0 . \label{eq:pendulum}
\end{align}
$$

At larger angles, the $\sin x$ term changes the motion. The period grows with the amplitude, and a pendulum that moves fast enough swings over the top. The exact solution involves elliptic functions, so in practice we solve the equation numerically. By the end of this post, we will have drawn the phase portrait of the pendulum in Fig. 1.

## A system of first-order ODEs

Equation $\eqref{eq:pendulum}$ is an example of a second-order, nonlinear ordinary differential equation (ODE). We can now introduce the angular velocity,

$$
\begin{align}
y & = \dfrac{d x}{d t},
\end{align}
$$

and convert the second-order ODE into a system of two first-order ODEs, viz.,

$$
\begin{align}
\dot{x} & = y, \\
\dot{y} & = - \sin x.
\end{align}
$$

Here, the dot denotes a derivative with respect to time. Numerical ODE solvers such as `odeint` work with systems of first-order equations, so we always rewrite a higher-order equation in this form first.

We can now introduce the state vector $\boldsymbol{S} = (x, y)$ and write our two-dimensional system as

$$
\begin{align}
\dfrac{d \boldsymbol{S}}{d t} =
\begin{pmatrix}
S_2 \\
-\sin S_1
\end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix}
y \\
-\sin x
\end{pmatrix} ,
\end{align}
$$

where $S_i$ is the $i$th component of $\boldsymbol{S}$.

Our task is to analyse the behaviour of this system, and we do that by

- drawing the phase portrait of the system,
- solving the system of ODEs numerically.

We shall use Python to perform the above tasks. We begin by importing suitable Python libraries,

- `numpy` to perform manipulations over numeric arrays
- `scipy.integrate.odeint` to numerically solve our ODEs
- `matplotlib` to plot the results.

If you do not have a Python environment with these packages yet, see the [[2026-07-06-setting-up-scientific-python-environment|guide to setting one up]]. For books and tutorials on scientific Python, see [[2026-07-06-pythonrefs]].

```python
import numpy as np  # arrays and vectorised maths
from scipy.integrate import odeint  # the ODE solver
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt  # plotting
```

Our first task is to code up a function that returns the right-hand side (RHS) of the system.

```python
def f(S, t=0):
    x, y = S  # unpack the state into the angle and the angular velocity
    return [y, -np.sin(x)]  # the RHS of the system, [x', y']
```

The function takes two arguments, the state `S` and the time `t`. The line `x, y = S` unpacks the state into its two components. Our system is autonomous, that is, its RHS does not depend on time. We still include `t`, because ODE solvers expect a function of both the dependent and the independent variables. The order of the arguments matters, and `odeint` expects the dependent variables first. We also give `t` the default value `0`, so that we can call `f` without it when we plot the vector field. The same function then serves to solve the ODEs and to plot the phase portrait.

## The phase portrait

Each point $(x, y)$ of the plane gives a state of the pendulum, its angle and its angular velocity. As time runs, the state moves along a curve in this plane, called a trajectory. The vector field $\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}$ gives the direction and the speed of the motion at every point. We call this plane the phase plane, and a sketch of the vector field with a few trajectories is a phase portrait.

Our first order of business is to generate the phase portrait: we have to essentially sketch the vector field

$$
\begin{align}
\dot{\boldsymbol{S}} = (\dot{x}, \dot{y}) = (y, -\sin x) .
\end{align}
$$

To plot a vector field, we need the following two functions:

- `numpy.meshgrid` to generate a grid of points (over the $xy$ plane) where we must calculate the value of the components of the vector field.
- `matplotlib.pyplot.streamplot` to visualize a vector field by plotting the streamlines of the vector flow.

```python
# a wide figure with one set of axes
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(40, 10))

xmin, xmax = -4.0 * np.pi, 4.0 * np.pi  # range of the angle x
ymin, ymax = -3.0, 3.0  # range of the angular velocity y

x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, 30)  # 30 points along each axis
y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, 30)

XX, YY = np.meshgrid(x, y)  # coordinates of every point of the grid

xdot, ydot = f([XX, YY])  # the vector field at every point of the grid

ax.streamplot(XX, YY, xdot, ydot, density=2)  # draw the streamlines

plt.show()
```

The primary purpose of using `meshgrid` is to avoid using slow Python loops.

<details class="prompt-fold" markdown="1">
<summary>How <code>numpy.meshgrid</code> works</summary>

`numpy.meshgrid` takes the points along each axis and returns the coordinates of every point of the grid they span. For three points along $x$ and two along $y$,

```python
x = np.linspace(0, 2, 3)  # [0, 1, 2]
y = np.linspace(0, 1, 2)  # [0, 1]
XX, YY = np.meshgrid(x, y)
```

gives

```text
XX = [[0, 1, 2],     YY = [[0, 0, 0],
      [0, 1, 2]]           [1, 1, 1]]
```

Each array has one row for each value of $y$ and one column for each value of $x$. The pair `(XX[i, j], YY[i, j])` is one point of the grid. NumPy applies arithmetic and functions such as `np.sin` element by element, so `f([XX, YY])` evaluates the vector field at every point of the grid in one call. Without `meshgrid`, we would need two nested loops over $x$ and $y$, and loops in Python run much more slowly.

</details>

Notice the line where `streamplot` is invoked. The basic syntax is:

`ax.streamplot(x_grid, y_grid, x_vec, y_vec, density=spacing)`

Here `x_grid` and `y_grid` are arrays of `x`, `y` points. The arrays `x_vec` and `y_vec` denote the stream velocity at each point on the grid. The keyword argument `density=spacing` specifies how close the streamlines are drawn together.

The fixed points of the system, where $\dot{\boldsymbol{S}} = 0$, lie at $y = 0$ and $\sin x = 0$, that is, at $x = n\pi$ for integer $n$. We find the type of each by linearising the equation about it. Near $x = 0$, $\sin x \approx x$ and $\ddot{x} = -x$. Here the pendulum hangs straight down and makes small oscillations about a stable equilibrium, and the streamlines circle the point. We call such a point a centre. Near $x = \pi$, we write $x = \pi + \xi$, so that $\sin x \approx -\xi$ and $\ddot{\xi} = \xi$, whose solutions grow or decay exponentially. Here the pendulum stands upright, balanced at an unstable equilibrium. The streamlines come in along one direction and leave along another, and we call such a point a saddle. Since $\sin x$ has period $2\pi$, every even multiple of $\pi$ is a centre and every odd multiple a saddle, as Fig. 2 shows.

## Solving the ODEs with `odeint`

We shall now use `odeint` to numerically solve the two-dimensional system.

Since we have two differential equations, we have to provide two initial conditions. First, we shall try to simulate the case where the bob of the pendulum starts at its lowest point and we set it into motion with some initial velocity, that is,

$$ x(0) = 0 , \quad y(0) = c , $$
where $c$ is a constant.

```python
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(40, 10))

t0, tf = 0.0, 45.0  # start and end of the time interval
# times at which odeint returns the solution
tspan = np.linspace(t0, tf, 1000)

x0 = 0.0  # every pendulum starts at the bottom

c = np.linspace(-2.05, 2.05, 10)  # initial angular velocities

for y0 in c:
    S0 = [x0, y0]  # initial state
    # one row for each time, with columns x and y
    ys = odeint(f, S0, tspan)
    # the trajectory in the phase plane
    ax.plot(ys[:, 0], ys[:, 1], 'r-')

plt.show()
```

Figure 3 shows the trajectories that this code computes. In the above code block, we first set up the figure and the axes, and the time interval over which we solve our ODEs. The array `tspan` holds 1000 equally spaced times between `t0` and `tf`, and `odeint` returns the solution at these times. We then fix the first initial condition, $x(0) = 0$, and construct an array of suitable values of $c$ for the second. The loop takes one value of $c$ at a time, calls the ODE solver with the initial conditions $[0, c]$, stores the solution in `ys` and plots it. The first column, `ys[:, 0]`, holds $x(t)$, and the second, `ys[:, 1]`, holds $y(t)$.

The value $\lvert c \rvert = 2$ comes from the energy of the pendulum. Multiplying Eq. $\eqref{eq:pendulum}$ by $\dot{x}$, we find

$$
\begin{align}
\dot{x}\ddot{x} + \dot{x} \sin x = \dfrac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{1}{2} \dot{x}^2 - \cos x \right) = 0 ,
\end{align}
$$

so the energy

$$
\begin{align}
E = \frac{1}{2} y^2 - \cos x
\end{align}
$$

stays constant along every trajectory. The trajectories in the phase portrait are therefore the level curves of $E$. A pendulum started at $x = 0$ with $y = c$ has ${E = c^2/2 - 1}$. It reaches the top, $x = \pm \pi$, only if $E \geq 1$, that is, if $\lvert c \rvert \geq 2$.

For $\lvert c \rvert < 2$ the bob swings back and forth, and the trajectories are closed curves around the origin. For $\lvert c \rvert > 2$ the bob has enough energy to swing over the top, and the trajectory runs off to the right if $c > 0$ and to the left if $c < 0$. The two ends of our array, $c = \pm 2.05$, belong to this second class. We avoid $c = \pm 2$ itself. A pendulum started there lies on the separatrix, the level curve $E = 1$, or $y = \pm 2 \cos(x/2)$. The separatrix passes through the saddles and divides the two kinds of motion, and a numerical solution cannot stay on it.

We have used `odeint` to solve our ODEs; the syntax is `ys = odeint(model, initial_values, t)`, where `model` is the RHS of the two-dimensional system, `initial_values` is an array of initial conditions, and `t` is an array of the times at which we want the solution.

`odeint` returns a matrix which has $k$ columns, one for each of the $k$ variables, and a row for each time point specified in `t`. `odeint` is SciPy's [\[3\]](#ref-3) interface to LSODA, a solver from the FORTRAN library ODEPACK [\[2\]](#ref-2) that switches automatically between methods for stiff and non-stiff equations.

### How `odeint` works

ODEPACK is a collection of FORTRAN solvers for initial value problems, written by Alan Hindmarsh and colleagues at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in the early 1980s [\[2\]](#ref-2). Its solvers grew out of Gear's work on stiff equations in the 1970s. They are still in use more than four decades later, and R's `deSolve` package and GNU Octave's `lsode` call them too. Linda Petzold and Hindmarsh added LSODA, the *Livermore Solver for Ordinary Differential equations with Automatic method switching*, to this family in 1983 [\[4\]](#ref-4).

LSODA uses two families of linear multistep methods. For non-stiff problems it uses the Adams–Moulton methods, of orders 1 to 12, which predict the next value from a polynomial through the previous steps and then correct it. For stiff problems it uses the backward differentiation formulas (BDF), of orders 1 to 5. These are implicit, so each step solves a nonlinear system by Newton's method. `odeint` estimates the Jacobian for this by finite differences, unless we pass a function for it through the argument `Dfun`. In both cases, the solver changes the step size and the order as it goes, to keep the estimated error of each step within the tolerances `rtol` and `atol`.

A system is stiff when it contains time scales that differ by many orders of magnitude. An Adams method then needs steps as small as the fastest time scale to stay stable, even where the solution itself changes slowly. A BDF method can take steps set by the accuracy alone. LSODA starts with the Adams methods and estimates, as it goes, the step size that each family could take. It switches to BDF when the problem turns stiff, and back to Adams when it stops being stiff.

We can ask LSODA which method it uses. With `full_output=True`, `odeint` also returns a dictionary of diagnostics. Its entry `mused` gives the method in use at each output time, 1 for Adams and 2 for BDF, `nqu` gives the order, and `tsw` gives the time of the last switch between the two. For the pendulum, with the output of each line in a comment,

```python
S0 = [0.0, 1.5]
ys, info = odeint(f, S0, tspan, full_output=True)

# methods in use, 1 for Adams and 2 for BDF
print(np.unique(info['mused']))  # [1]
# highest order used
print(info['nqu'].max())  # 9
# time of the last switch between methods
print(info['tsw'][-1])  # 0.0
```

LSODA never leaves the Adams methods and reaches order 9, as we expect for a system that is not stiff. To see why the pendulum is not stiff, we linearise it about a point $(x, y)$ of a trajectory. The Jacobian of the RHS, the matrix of its partial derivatives, is

$$
\begin{align}
J =
\begin{pmatrix}
\partial \dot{x} / \partial x & \partial \dot{x} / \partial y \\
\partial \dot{y} / \partial x & \partial \dot{y} / \partial y
\end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
-\cos x & 0
\end{pmatrix} .
\end{align}
$$

Its eigenvalues solve ${\det(J - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + \cos x = 0}$, so ${\lambda = \pm \sqrt{-\cos x}}$. Each eigenvalue sets a local time scale of the motion, $1 / \lvert \lambda \rvert$. Near the bottom, $\cos x \approx 1$ and $\lambda = \pm i$, an oscillation with the period $2\pi$. Near the top, $\cos x \approx -1$ and $\lambda = \pm 1$, a growth or decay at the rate 1. Since $\lvert \cos x \rvert \leq 1$, no eigenvalue exceeds 1 in size anywhere in the phase plane. The pendulum has no time scale much shorter than 1, so it is not stiff.

The switch is a heuristic, though. For the rotation started at $x = 5\pi$ with $y = -1$, solved at the 4001 output times of the final script, LSODA uses BDF from $t \approx 40.2$ to $41.8$ and then returns to Adams. For contrast, consider the van der Pol oscillator, $\ddot{x} - \mu (1 - x^2) \dot{x} + x = 0$, with $\mu = 1000$, a standard stiff test problem. Its solution creeps slowly for long stretches and then jumps quickly, twice in each period of about ${(3 - 2 \ln 2) \mu \approx 1614}$ [\[1\]](#ref-1).

```python
def vdp(S, t, mu=1000.0):
    x, y = S
    return [y, mu * (1 - x**2) * y - x]


t = np.linspace(0.0, 3000.0, 300001)
ys, info = odeint(vdp, [2.0, 0.0], t, full_output=True)

print(np.unique(info['tsw']).round(3).tolist())  # times of the switches
# [0.005, 807.077, 807.095, 1614.276, 1614.294, 2421.476, 2421.493]
```

LSODA switches to BDF almost at once, at $t = 0.005$. At each jump, near $t = 807$, $1614$ and $2421$, the problem stops being stiff for a moment. LSODA then switches to Adams for about $0.02$ units of time, and back to BDF when the slow motion resumes.

<details class="prompt-fold" markdown="1">
<summary><code>odeint</code> and <code>solve_ivp</code></summary>

SciPy also provides [`solve_ivp`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.integrate.solve_ivp.html), a newer function for initial value problems, and the documentation of [`odeint`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.integrate.odeint.html) recommends it for new code. The two functions differ as follows:

- `solve_ivp` expects the right-hand side as `f(t, S)`, with the time first. `odeint` expects `f(S, t)`, unless we pass `tfirst=True`.
- `solve_ivp` takes the time interval `(t0, tf)`, and we pass the times at which we want the solution in `t_eval`.
- `solve_ivp` lets us choose the method, among them `RK45` (the default), `DOP853`, `Radau`, `BDF` and `LSODA`. `odeint` always uses LSODA.
- `solve_ivp` has much looser default tolerances (`rtol=1e-3`) than `odeint` (about `1.5e-8`), so we pass `rtol` and `atol` to get the same accuracy.
- `solve_ivp` returns an object that holds the solution in `sol.y`, with one row for each variable. `odeint` returns the transpose of this matrix.

With `solve_ivp`, we would compute the trajectories above as

```python
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

sol = solve_ivp(
    lambda t, S: f(S, t),
    (t0, tf),
    S0,
    t_eval=tspan,
    rtol=1e-8,
    atol=1e-8,
)
x, y = sol.y
```

</details>

## Putting it all together

Lastly, I will define a helper function to plot arrows along the trajectories on the phase space. The detailed working of this function is not important. It draws small arrowheads at regular intervals along a trajectory, each pointing in the direction of motion.

<details class="code-fold" markdown="1">
<summary>Show <code>plotarrows</code></summary>

```python
def plotarrows(xs, ys):
    # split the trajectory into 100 groups of s points
    s = int((xs.size) / 100)
    # drop the first point of each group
    xs = xs.reshape(-1, s)[:, 1:].flatten()
    ys = ys.reshape(-1, s)[:, 1:].flatten()

    for i in range(0, xs.size, 2):  # take the remaining points in pairs
        dx = xs[i + 1] - xs[i]  # the step from one point to the next
        dy = ys[i + 1] - ys[i]
        ax.arrow(  # an arrowhead pointing along this step
            xs[i],
            ys[i],
            dx,
            dy,
            shape='full',
            lw=0,
            length_includes_head=True,
            head_width=0.06,
            color='#ee5f5b',
        )
```

</details>

```python
    plotarrows(ys[:, 0], ys[:, 1])

ax.set_xlim(xmin, xmax)  # keep the window of the vector field
ax.set_ylim(ymin, ymax)

# uncomment for grid lines but no labels
# ax.set(yticklabels=[]) # hide y labels
# ax.set(xticklabels=[]) # hide x labels

plt.axis('off')  # hide the axes, ticks and labels

plt.show()
```

## Checking energy conservation

The exact solution conserves the energy $E$, so $E$ gives us a direct check on the numerical solution. Any change in $E$ along a computed trajectory is numerical error. The code below computes $E$ along three trajectories that start at $x = 0$, and plots $\lvert E(t) - E(0) \rvert$ on a logarithmic scale.

```python
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 4))

t0, tf = 0.0, 45.0
tspan = np.linspace(t0, tf, 1000)

x0 = 0.0

for y0 in [0.5, 1.5, 1.95]:  # three swings of increasing size
    S0 = [x0, y0]
    ys = odeint(f, S0, tspan)
    # the energy at each time
    E = 0.5 * ys[:, 1] ** 2 - np.cos(ys[:, 0])
    # its change, without t = 0
    ax.semilogy(tspan[1:], np.abs(E[1:] - E[0]), label=f'c = {y0}')

ax.set_xlabel('t')
ax.set_ylabel('|E(t) - E(0)|')
ax.legend()

plt.show()
```

![Energy error along three trajectories of the pendulum](/assets/images/nonlinearpendulum/energy-dark.png){: .dark width="1603" height="523" }

Figure 4 shows the result. We skip $t = 0$, where the error vanishes and its logarithm does not exist. With the default tolerances, the error stays below $3 \times 10^{-7}$ over the whole run. The error does not die away. LSODA keeps the error of each step small, and these errors add up over many steps. For $c = 0.5$ and $c = 1.5$, the typical error grows by a factor of three to five over the run. The sharp dips mark the moments when $E(t) - E(0)$ changes sign. With `rtol=1e-12` and `atol=1e-12`, the error falls below $2 \times 10^{-10}$. Symplectic integrators, such as the leapfrog method, keep the energy error bounded for all time, but they need a fixed step size.

### Friction

A real pendulum loses energy to friction. The simplest model adds a damping term proportional to the angular velocity,

$$
\begin{align}
\ddot{x} + \gamma \dot{x} + \sin x = 0 , \label{eq:damped}
\end{align}
$$

with $\gamma > 0$, so that the second equation of our system becomes $\dot{y} = -\sin x - \gamma y$. Multiplying by $\dot{x}$ as before, we now find $dE/dt = -\gamma y^2 \leq 0$, so friction drains the energy whenever the pendulum moves. Near the bottom, the linearised equation $\ddot{x} + \gamma \dot{x} + x = 0$ has solutions that oscillate with the frequency $\sqrt{1 - \gamma^2/4}$ inside the envelope $e^{-\gamma t / 2}$, so the centres turn into stable spirals. A pendulum that starts with enough energy to whirl over the top loses some of it on every turn. When it can no longer pass the top, one of the wells captures it, and it spirals to rest at $x = 2\pi n$.

Add the damping term to `f`, with $\gamma = 0.25$, and reproduce Fig. 5.

### The period of the pendulum

The period of the linear pendulum, $2\pi$, does not depend on the amplitude. The period of the nonlinear pendulum does. A pendulum started at $x = 0$ with $y = c$ swings up to the amplitude $x_{\mathrm{max}} = 2 \arcsin(c/2)$, and the conservation of energy gives its period in closed form,

$$
\begin{align}
T = 4 K(k) , \quad k = \frac{c}{2} ,
\end{align}
$$

where

$$
\begin{align}
K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1 - k^2 \sin^2 \varphi}}
\end{align}
$$

is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. For small swings, $K \to \pi/2$ and $T \to 2\pi$. As $c \to 2$, $K$ grows like $\ln \left( 4 / \sqrt{1 - k^2} \right)$, and the period becomes infinite on the separatrix, where the pendulum takes infinitely long to creep up to the top.

Plot $x(t)$ for a small and a large value of $c$. Then measure the period from the numerical solution for many values of $c$, and compare it with $4K(c/2)$. SciPy provides $K$ as `scipy.special.ellipk`, which takes the parameter $m = k^2$. Reproduce Fig. 6.

![The swing of the pendulum for c = 0.5 and c = 1.95, and its period against c compared with 4K(c/2)](/assets/images/nonlinearpendulum/pendulum-period-dark.png){: .dark width="1603" height="523" }

## Further reading

### Dynamical systems

The books below run roughly from introductory to advanced.

- S. H. Strogatz, [*Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3907581W), 2nd ed. (Westview Press, Boulder, CO, 2015). Secs. 6.3 and 6.5 cover fixed points, linearisation and conservative systems. Sec. 6.7 treats the pendulum, with and without damping, along the same lines as this post, and Sec. 7.5 treats the relaxation oscillations of the van der Pol oscillator.
- R. C. Hilborn, [*Chaos and Nonlinear Dynamics: An Introduction for Scientists and Engineers*](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198507239.001.0001), 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, Oxford, 2000). Chapter 3 treats dynamics in a state space of one and two dimensions, the setting of this post, and Chapter 8 treats Hamiltonian systems. The other chapters go on to iterated maps, quasi-periodicity and the measures that quantify chaos.
- D. D. Nolte, [*Introduction to Modern Dynamics: Chaos, Networks, Space, and Time*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198844624.001.0001), 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, Oxford, 2019). Nolte builds dynamics on the geometry of phase space. Chapter 3 treats Hamiltonian dynamics and phase space, Chapter 4 nonlinear dynamics and chaos, and Chapter 5 Hamiltonian chaos. Later chapters apply the same ideas to synchronization, networks and relativity.
- E. Ott, [*Chaos in Dynamical Systems*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511803260), 2nd ed. (Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, 2002). Ott writes for physicists and goes further than Strogatz. Chapter 3 treats strange attractors and fractal dimension, and Chapter 7 treats chaos in Hamiltonian systems, the class to which the pendulum without friction belongs.
- M. Brin and G. Stuck, [*Introduction to Dynamical Systems*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511755316) (Cambridge University Press, Cambridge, 2002). Brin and Stuck write for mathematicians and prove what they state. The book covers topological and symbolic dynamics, ergodic theory and hyperbolic dynamics, the theory behind the chaos in the books above.

### Classical mechanics

The books below run roughly from introductory to advanced.

- D. Morin, [*Introduction to Classical Mechanics: With Problems and Solutions*](https://www.cambridge.org/9780521876223) (Cambridge University Press, 2008). Sec. 5.1 uses the conservation of energy to treat motion in one dimension, the method behind the energy and the period of the pendulum in this post, and Sec. 5.2 treats small oscillations about an equilibrium. Secs. 4.3 and 4.4 treat damped and driven oscillators.
- S. T. Thornton and J. B. Marion, [*Classical Dynamics of Particles and Systems*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL21001752W), 5th ed. (Brooks/Cole, 2004). Chapter 4, on nonlinear oscillations and chaos, discusses the plane pendulum and its phase diagram at an introductory level.
- T. W. B. Kibble and F. H. Berkshire, [*Classical Mechanics*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL5266838W), 5th ed. (Imperial College Press, 2004). The book takes a geometric view of dynamics. Secs. 13.1 and 13.3 introduce phase space and the phase plane, Sec. 13.5 treats limit cycles, and Chapter 14 goes on to order and chaos in Hamiltonian systems.
- W. Greiner, [*Classical Mechanics: Systems of Particles and Hamiltonian Dynamics*](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-03434-3), 2nd ed. (Springer, 2010). Its chapters on dynamical systems and on bifurcations, in the part on nonlinear dynamics, take the ideas of this post to general flows in phase space.
- H. Goldstein, C. Poole, and J. Safko, [*Classical Mechanics*](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/classical-mechanics/P200000006871/9780201657029), 3rd ed. (Addison-Wesley, 2002). Chapter 11, on classical chaos, goes from periodic motion and the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem (Secs. 11.1 and 11.2) to attractors and Liapunov exponents (Secs. 11.3 and 11.4).
- J. V. José and E. J. Saletan, [*Classical Dynamics: A Contemporary Approach*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL19349641W) (Cambridge University Press, 1998). Chapter 7 treats nonlinear dynamics, and its Sec. 7.1, on nonlinear oscillators, includes the van der Pol oscillator.
- L. D. Landau and E. M. Lifshitz, [*Mechanics*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL26316759W), 3rd ed. (Pergamon, Oxford, 1976). Sec. 11 integrates motion in one dimension with the conservation of energy, and its first problem derives the period of the pendulum in terms of the elliptic integral $K$.
- K. S. Thorne and R. D. Blandford, [*Modern Classical Physics: Optics, Fluids, Plasmas, Elasticity, Relativity, and Statistical Physics*](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL21808419W) (Princeton University Press, 2017). Sec. 15.6 follows the route from regular flow to chaos in fluids, through Feigenbaum's period-doubling sequence, and Sec. 15.6.4 discusses the extreme sensitivity to initial conditions. These sections show where the ideas of this post lead in continuum physics.
